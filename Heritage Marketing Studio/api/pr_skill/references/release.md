# The release, part by part

A worked reference for the eight parts. The studio's gate checks that each exists and that the lead
answers its five questions; this is how to make each one worth having.

## Headline

The news, in the words a reader would use. Not the words the company uses in meetings.

- **Bad:** "Heritage Foods reinforces its commitment to quality excellence"
- **Better:** "Heritage tests every milk batch at collection, publishes the daily log"

The test: does it contain something that could be false? "Commitment to excellence" cannot be false, so
it is not news. "Publishes the daily log" can be checked, so it is.

## Dateline

`HYDERABAD, 24 August 2026 —`

Place and date. It tells a desk whose story this is geographically, which decides which edition picks it
up. For a multi-state story, dateline the place the news actually happened, not head office.

## Lead

Five questions, and the gate checks each separately because prose hides omissions.

| field | what it wants | the way it gets fudged |
|---|---|---|
| `who` | The company, and a person if one is central | Padding with the full legal entity name |
| `what` | What happened or was established, no adjectives | "Announced an initiative" — an announcement is not an event |
| `when` | The date it happened or takes effect | "Recently" |
| `where` | Place at the specificity the story needs | Head office, when the news happened at a plant |
| `why` | Why a reader **outside** the company should care | Why the company is pleased about it |

`why` is the one that fails. "As part of our continued commitment" is not a reason for a reader to care.
"Because loose milk sold in the same street is not tested at all" is.

## Support

The numbers, and where they come from. One paragraph. This is where a figure belongs — not the lead,
which needs one number at most.

If a number cannot be sourced, it does not go in. A journalist who cannot verify a figure either drops
it or attributes it as a company claim, which weakens the whole piece.

## Quote

A named person, their role, and a sentence they would say out loud.

- **Bad:** "We are delighted to announce this industry-leading initiative which underscores our
  unwavering dedication to consumer trust."
- **Better:** "We were already testing every batch. Publishing the log just means you can check it
  without asking us."

Two tests:
1. **The competitor test.** Could a competitor's CEO have said this about their own company? If yes, it
   says nothing.
2. **The read-aloud test.** Would a person be embarrassed to say it in a room? If yes, it is a slogan in
   quotation marks and a journalist will cut it.

A quote is the one place opinion is legitimate, because it is attributed. Put the adjectives here.

## Background

Context a reader may need and most will not. Company history, category context, the previous chapter of
the story. Last, because it is the first thing an editor cuts — and if losing it damages the piece, it
was in the wrong place.

## Boilerplate

Three sentences that never change: what the company is, its scale, since when. Written once, reused
verbatim, updated deliberately.

Keep the scale figures current. A boilerplate quoting a farmer count from four years ago is the kind of
error a journalist finds and mentions.

## Contact

A name, a mobile number, and an email that will be answered **today**. Not `press@`, not a switchboard.

A release with a contact nobody answers is a story that gets written without you, using whatever the
reporter could find.

## Embargo

Only when there is a genuine reason — a simultaneous announcement, a market disclosure, an event. State
it plainly at the top with a date and a time including the zone.

An embargo on something that is not sensitive is an irritation that gets ignored, and once one of your
embargoes has been broken without consequence, none of them work again.

## Length

300 to 400 words across the whole thing. Under is fine if nothing a journalist needs is missing. Over
about 400 it gets skimmed — and what is below the quote is almost always what should go.

Count the words before deciding the release is finished, not after somebody asks.
