# The media map

Who to reach, in which language, and why that one. Short and current beats long and dead.

## The five tiers, and what each wants

| tier | what it is | what it wants | what it will not take |
|---|---|---|---|
| `wire` | PTI, ANI and the like | Clean facts, no angle, fast | Anything that needs interpretation |
| `national` | National English dailies and business press | The sharpest angle, and a national frame | A story that only matters in one state |
| `language` | State-language mass press | The story as it affects readers *here*, in their idiom | A translated English pitch |
| `trade` | Category press, read by the channel and the competitor | Numbers, capacity, distribution, margin | Consumer emotion |
| `digital` | Digital news | Linkable, quotable, quick | A PDF |

**A wire pickup is not one item of coverage.** One PTI story syndicates into dozens of titles. Counting
it as a single mention understates it badly, which is why the studio files wires as their own tier and
excludes them from circulation totals — a wire carries no circulation of its own.

## Lead with the language press

For most Indian readers, attitudes are formed in the state-language press, not in English. And the
markets are concentrated: in every Indian language market the top two titles hold more than half the
readership. Eenadu and Sakshi together reach roughly 71% of Telugu readers. Four Hindi titles hold about
76%.

Two consequences:

1. **A good list is short.** If eight titles cover the majority of the audience in a state, a list of
   sixty is padding.
2. **Under-serving this tier is the commonest and least excusable error**, usually because nobody in the
   room reads those papers. The fix is not effort, it is attention.

## The record, and why every field is there

| field | why |
|---|---|
| language, tier | Decides what the pitch is and who writes it |
| edition, region | A masthead is not one newspaper; an edition decides whether the story runs |
| category | Trade titles are category-specific; a dairy trade monthly does not want a cement story |
| circulation + source + date | The only defensible print figure, and it means nothing without its source |
| digital + source + date | A different audience, measured differently. Never added to circulation |
| confirmed | Whether a person has checked the masthead exists and is what the list says |
| verified | When anyone last checked. Records go stale, and the date is the only way to see it |

## Freshness

Journalism turns over fast. Four bands, and `unverified` is separate from `stale` on purpose — a row
nobody ever checked is not an old fact, it is not a fact.

- **fresh** (0–90 days) — usable.
- **ageing** (90–270) — verify before pitching. Beats move quietly.
- **stale** (270+) — assume the desk, the beat or the person has changed.
- **unverified** — a lead, not a contact.

## Journalists

**Never invent one.** A name, a beat and a contact are either known or missing, and a missing record
should say so. A fabricated reporter is worse than an empty row in two ways: the pitch goes nowhere, and
it is an invention about a real profession that no reader of the list can detect.

What a good record carries beyond the name:

- the **beat**, confirmed over a few months of their output rather than from a job title;
- **what they last wrote that is relevant** — this is what makes a pitch personal, and personalisation
  roughly triples response;
- how they prefer to be reached. Almost universally: one-to-one email. Not a DM, not a phone call.

## Pitching, in one paragraph

A journalist reads the first line and decides. Reference something they actually wrote, say what the
news is in one sentence, say why their readers specifically should care, and offer the thing that makes
it easy to cover — the data, the access, the picture, the soundbite. Then stop.

The studio prepares the pitch. A named person sends it. Volume outreach is the single thing journalists
complain about most, and it burns the only asset this whole discipline runs on.
