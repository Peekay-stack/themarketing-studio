# Brand characters

A segmentation study or a pen portrait settles **who this is for**. It does not settle **who the brand
puts in front of them, again and again, until that face is the brand's own.** That gap is where a
perfectly good persona becomes a fresh stock photo every time someone opens the studio — six pieces get
cast separately and no two of them look like the same person, so nothing accumulates.

A character is the thing that closes that gap. It sits in the sequence like this:

> **Segmentation study (or a pen portrait, if none exists) → Visual brief → Cast → Pressure test → Approved brand character → reused until retired**
> *(a plan decides who a piece is briefed for — it does not decide who plays them)*

## Persona, not character

The distinction is the whole point, and getting it wrong is why most "brand personas" never get
recognized twice.

| | Persona | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Lifespan | one brief | years |
| Yields | targeting parameters | a face, a gesture, a prop, a world |
| Built on | demographics | a **signature visual hook** |
| Test | does it describe someone? | would a person recognise them with the logo covered? |

*"25–34, urban, working mother, mid-income"* is a persona. It generates a casting brief once and is
forgotten the moment the piece ships.

*"The mother who pours the first glass before anyone else is awake, in the same steel tumbler every
time"* is a character. It generates a hook slide, a POS hero, a video protagonist and a carousel closer —
same person, same tumbler, five pieces, none of them a stock photo standing in for the idea.

**If you cannot name the hook, you have a persona.** Write the pen portrait down anyway — it is real,
useful work — but do not cast it as a recurring character yet, because everything downstream will expect
it to be recognisable and it will not be.

The hook does not have to be an object. A hairstyle worn one exact way, a wardrobe palette that is hers
alone, a way of holding her chin when she smiles — any of these lock identity as well as a prop does, and
for most brand characters the fuller visual identity (styling, grooming, expression range held together)
carries more of the recognition than any single signature object ever will. Reach for an object or gesture
when one genuinely fits; do not force one onto a character whose real hook is simply how she is styled and
how she carries herself.

## The six tests

Apply these before a character is approved. A character that fails one is not frozen — it goes back a
stage, named which one failed.

1. **It has a named, honestly-labelled pen portrait.** Say out loud whether it is built on the brand's
   own segmentation research or is a working hypothesis with none behind it yet. An assumption dressed as
   research is how a persona becomes a stereotype nobody notices until a real customer points it out.
2. **It has a signature visual hook.** One specific, repeatable detail — a gesture, an object, a styling
   choice, a way of standing — that is this character's alone. Ask: *could a competitor's brand use this
   exact description and it would still fit their ad?* If yes, there is no hook yet, only a demographic.
3. **It is specific and presentable — not specific instead of presentable.** Build from particular,
   ownable details — a distinct bone structure, a hairstyle, a way of standing — never generic beauty. But
   specific is not a codeword for unglamorous. Cast and style this character the way a real brand
   ambassador is cast: real makeup, real hair, real wardrobe craft, lit and posed the way a consumer would
   actually want to see them again. The failure this test exists to catch is the idealised-into-blandness
   AI-default face with no distinguishing feature — not attractiveness itself. Think about what the
   consumer wants to see, not what feels most authentic to show them; a character can be memorably specific
   and still be exactly the person a viewer is glad turned up in their feed.
4. **It holds identity across contexts, checked before it is frozen.** Render the same locked candidate
   in at least three genuinely different situations — a different setting, a different producer's
   format, a different mood — before approving it. A face that only survives the one staged photo it was
   drafted in is not yet a character; it is a lucky render.
5. **It is framed as a character, never a testimonial.** No first-person product claims, no "real
   customer says" framing, no implied endorsement. This is a real line, not a style note — a fictional
   character quoted as if giving a genuine testimonial is the specific case advertising-standards bodies
   treat as high-risk regardless of any AI disclosure, and it is also the fastest way to lose the trust a
   recurring character is supposed to build.
6. **It is brand-scoped, never shared.** Built for one brand's segments. A character does not cross
   tenants, does not get reused for a different brand's campaign, and is not a generic library face with
   a brand's logo dropped beside it.

### The failure that actually happens: specific *instead of* presentable

Test 3 is the one first drafts fail, and they fail it in a predictable direction — the model reads
"build from ownable, particular detail" as licence to make the character plain, and returns a face with
a scar, an un-styled look and an unsmiling mouth, mistaking unglamour for authenticity. That was a real
first-round outcome on the first character built with this skill; the second version fixed it, and the
fix is worth stating as a rule because the instinct is to reach for the wrong one.

When a draft comes back plain, **do not soften the hook and do not trade away the specificity.** Instead:

- **Keep every ownable structural detail** — the bone structure, the hairline, the one distinguishing
  mark. Those are what hold identity across renders; losing them to "make her prettier" is how the
  character becomes the generic-average face again.
- **Re-cast the styling upward** to where a real brand actually puts an ambassador — real hair and
  makeup, wardrobe craft, a finished (not blurred) skin, lit and posed the way a consumer would be glad
  to see again.
- **If the brief is for a specific regional market, make region load-bearing in the same pass.** A draft
  that failed "presentable" has almost always also let the region go generic — name it concretely
  (Telugu, Hyderabad — not "Indian") — because both gaps have one cause: the model filling an
  under-specified slot with its own default.

"Specific" and "glamorous" are not in tension. The character can be memorably particular and still be
exactly the person a viewer is glad turned up in their feed. That is the target, not a compromise
between the two.

## How to work

**Write the pen portrait first, structured, not a paragraph.** Background, demographics, a day-in-the-life
snapshot (when they wake, what they do before anyone else is up, what they carry, what they worry about),
goals, challenges, and motivations split into head (logical) and heart (emotional). If real segmentation
research exists, ground it there and cite it. If it does not, write the hypothesis in full and mark it as
one — this is not a lesser step, it is the honest version of the same step.

**Translate the portrait into a visual brief before casting anyone — and write it as a real casting brief,
not a physical inventory.** Professional casting separates two questions, and this brief should too.
**Type** — the energy and presence a viewer should read in one phrase: warm-but-firm, quietly formidable,
playful and a little sly. Commercial casting is type-driven; the working question is "are you the type?",
not "are you the character?", and a brief with a physical stat line and no type line is the one that comes
back generically pleasant instead of specific. The type line is not invented fresh — it is the pen
portrait's head/heart goals, said once in casting language. **Look** — age, build, hair, makeup, wardrobe
palette and styling signature, the one distinguishing detail. Then setting, props, mood and light, and an
expression range, not one fixed expression. This is the actual casting prompt — a pen portrait alone is
not enough to cast from, the same way a messaging house alone is not enough to brief a shoot from.

**Region is load-bearing when it is the point, never a placeholder.** Default to leaving ethnicity and
region open — most briefs do not need it named. But a brand character built for a specific regional market
is exactly the case where it stops being optional, the same way professional casting directors specialise
by language and region rather than treating a whole country as one pool. Name the actual geography and
community concretely — Telugu, Telangana, not "Indian"; Bengali, Kolkata, not "Indian" — because vague
regional language fails the same way vague physical language does: the model fills the gap with its own
generic default, and "specific to a market" quietly becomes "generic to a continent."

**Fit the wardrobe to what must actually be verified.** If the brief claims a build or fitness trait, at
least one canonical framing must put it in fitted, not loose, clothing — a real production runs a physical
wardrobe fitting before the shoot for exactly this reason: a trait the silhouette hides might as well not
have been written. A loose kurta and "toned, fit build" in the same brief is not a contradiction the model
will resolve for you; it will simply draw the kurta and let the claim go unverified.

**Write the cast lock as two lists, not one description.** *Never changes*: face, build, and the one or
two identity-anchoring details (a birthmark, a hairline, a bone structure) that must survive every render.
*Changes freely*: wardrobe, hairstyle-of-the-day, setting, time of day — and always, mood and expression.
Expression is context, not a trait; it should move with the moment the way a real person's face does,
never get locked at the casting stage the way a name or a build is.

**Cast in candidates raw first, styled second — the order a modelling agency actually uses.** Generate the
first round with minimal makeup, plain neutral light, no wardrobe styling: the equivalent of a Polaroid, sent
specifically so a client judges identity on its own, not on top of hair and makeup already doing some of the
work for it. Pick from that round on bone structure and face alone. Only then apply the full visual brief —
glam, wardrobe, styling — to the one candidate chosen. A face picked while already wearing its final styling
is a face nobody actually evaluated on its own; two or three raw drafts, exactly like a key visual's
treatment routes — a person picks one, the other two are not wasted, they are the record of what was
considered.

**Audition the turn before pressure-testing contexts.** Generate a short video turn — profile left, centre,
profile right — framed exactly as if the character is presenting themselves for selection as the brand's
own AI ambassador. A single static image can look right and still not hold as one identity once a video
model has to move it: the face softens, drifts toward someone else, or reveals there was never one
coherent identity underneath. A candidate that fails this turn fails here, before spending a single frame
on a real context, and goes back to casting.

**Pressure-test before freezing.** See test 4. This is the step a single "looks good, approve it" click
skips, and it is the one that catches a character that only works in the render it was born in.

**Approve explicitly.** A character is not ground truth until a person says so — same discipline as every
other asset in this studio. Nothing here defaults to approved.

**Know which stage the character is at.** The studio tracks it as a state, and it moves one step at a
time:

> draft → candidate → audition → pressure → approved   *(→ retired)*

*draft* is a written portrait and brief, nothing cast. *candidate* has a named hook, a **confirmed**
route and a Type line. *audition* means the turn has been generated. *pressure* means the
multi-context test is running. *approved* is a gate, not a label — it requires **all six tests
passing**, a **signed-off reference frame in the reference library** (a `cast` item), and a **named
person**; missing any one, approval is refused with the checklist of what's absent. A character that
fails a test drops back a stage *with that test named*, it does not sit frozen. **A producer's
cast-lock is only ever offered an `approved` character** — a candidate is not castable, however good
the one render it was born in looks.

## Prompting for image and video

The better the cue, the more the generator holds one identity instead of drifting toward its own generic
default on every new render. This is not a taste preference — it is the mechanism behind test 3: vague
adjectives (*attractive, happy, professional*) give the model nothing to lock onto and it fills the gap
with the same idealised-average face every brand ends up with. Concrete, sensory detail is what a
generator can actually hold onto across many separate generations.

Write prompts with real vocabulary, not summary words:

- **Light** — name the source and its quality: soft window daylight, a tube light against a grey window,
  golden hour through a doorway, a studio softbox. "Well lit" locks nothing; "tube light against daylight,
  slightly cool" does.
- **Lens and framing** — waist-up, three-quarter, profile, wide establishing, close on the hands. The
  character's cast sheet should specify which framings it has been proven to hold in the pressure test,
  not leave every render to guess.
- **Material and texture** — the weave of a fabric, the specific wear on a surface, skin texture, the way
  loose hair falls versus hair that is clipped up. Texture is what separates a rendered person from a
  waxy one. But wear has two readings, and only one belongs here: a patina, a polish worn bright where
  hands touch daily, a softened fold in cloth — reads as loved and used, the heirloom cue that makes a
  prop feel real. A dent, a crack, a stain, visible disrepair — reads as damaged and neglected, and
  undoes presentable the same way an unglamorous face did in test 3. Name the former; negative-prompt
  the latter explicitly, the same as any other genericness this character is not.
- **Colour and grade** — a named palette (warm brass and cotton-white, not "warm tones"), and whether the
  reference is a flat, true-colour product shot or a graded, moody film look.
- **Negative prompt, always** — name the generic-default failure directly: glamour lighting, studio
  backdrop, idealised model, glossy skin, smiling at camera (when the brief calls for a candid, unposed
  moment) — whatever the specific genericness this character is not.

Video prompts carry everything the image prompt carries, plus **motion and duration of the moment** — a
slow turn, not a pose; a hand reaching for something and completing the reach, not freezing mid-gesture.
Treat the audition turn (above) as the template for every subsequent video generation of this character:
same framing discipline, same insistence that the identity survive movement, not just a single frame.

## Expressing it

For each producer, one honest sentence about what the character *is* there — not a redescription of the
pen portrait, and never invented fresh per piece.

What every producer's cast-lock actually receives is a single **identity-only block**: the hook, the
Type and Look, the "never changes" list, and the standing negative. The scene, the wardrobe of the day
and — always — the expression are the producer's to set for that piece; they are never read off the
character sheet, because a character frozen into one expression stops being able to act. If a producer
needs to change anything in the identity block to make a piece work, that is a finding about the
character, not a per-piece edit.

- **Social / Carousel** — the recurring face across an ordered story or a run of posts: same person, same
  hook, different mornings. The character's locked reference is what every slide's cast lock points at —
  it is never redrafted per post.
- **POS material** — the cast reference composited into the hero cut-out, same as any other locked
  reference. The character's identity is never re-described from scratch per format; only the scene
  changes.
- **Video** — the character is the protagonist across script beats, replacing what a film currently
  builds fresh each time as a throwaway character sheet. The same locked identity carries from logline to
  final cut.
- **Onground** — usually does not apply. An activation is a real promoter doing a real thing in a real
  street; casting a fictional character into that role would misstate what actually happened there. If a
  character appears at all here, it is in the communication *about* the activation, not as the promoter.
- **PR** — does not apply. A fictional character is never quoted as a spokesperson or a source. This is
  the sharpest version of test 5, not a separate rule.

If a character genuinely has nothing to add in a medium, say that. An empty expression is a finding; an
invented one is a character wearing a testimonial's clothes.

## Casting an ensemble

A brand's cast is usually more than one person — a family, a household, a recurring pair. The same
pipeline runs once per member, in full; casting one well does not lower the bar for the next.

**Every member needs their own Type and their own hook.** A family should read as individuals who happen
to be related, not variations on one template. Test it directly: could two members' hook or type line be
swapped and nobody would notice? If yes, neither is differentiated yet — sharpen both, not just the one
that felt weaker.

**Cast the whole ensemble in the same world.** Region, era and social register apply uniformly — the same
market, the same specificity discipline, the same level of glam-versus-plain — unless a deliberate contrast
between members is the actual point. A modern daughter against a traditionalist grandmother is a real,
chosen contrast; an accidentally inconsistent register across the family is just an unforced error.

**Family resemblance is optional texture, never a substitute for a hook.** Real families share fragments —
a jaw, a laugh, a way of standing — and sharing one is a nice, additive detail. It is never a reason to skip
giving each member their own signature hook: shared features make a family recognisable as a family, not
each person recognisable as themselves, and this skill's whole point is the latter.

**Each member is approved on their own, not by association.** A well-loved lead character does not
grandfather a weaker supporting one through the six tests. Run the full pipeline — pen portrait, visual
brief, raw check, cast, audition turn, pressure test, approval — per person, every time.

## Style

Specific, not decorative. A character description that needs a paragraph to state the hook has probably
buried it in adjectives — the hook should be nameable in one clause, the same way a platform's mechanic
should be nameable in a sentence.

The same discipline applies to the whole brief, not just the hook: real casting breakdowns stay short on
purpose — physical detail limited to what is actually critical, one line of type, one line of scenario —
because a breakdown that is too long will not be read, and one that is nothing but physical descriptors
will not attract the right candidate either. More fields are not more rigor; they are more places for the
point to get lost.

Mark sources: `[segmentation]` for research-backed detail, `[pen-portrait]` for a working hypothesis not
yet validated, `[brand]` for anything pulled from the existing brand profile, or `[assumed]` for anything
supplied without either.
